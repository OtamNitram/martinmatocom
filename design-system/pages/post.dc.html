The armor I called confidence.

Written by Martín MatoPrincipal Software Engineer at Kaizen Softworks · About me
In this story · 6 parts

  2. A second chance
  3. No titles to lean on
  4. Breaking the armor
  5. Where I am today

01The layoff list

Nine months into my first job as a junior developer, I was called into a meeting nobody wants to have. I was at the top of the layoff list. My manager at the time was the one who gave me the news. Even though I reacted politely, I was angry. I asked for the specific reason, although part of me didn’t want to hear it. I tried to defend myself, but the decision had already been made. I thought the company was unfair, the world was unfair and all the other things I imagine you’ve heard before. I was blaming everyone and everything instead of looking at my part of the problem.

So, what was my part? My team was a small one at an e-banking solutions company. The SDLC process was far from perfect, and the onboarding wasn’t ideal either. But I worked the only way I knew: sit down, put on my headphones and code. It was me, the code and the process. Sometimes other people’s answers just weren’t enough for me. Taking off my headphones to ask for help felt like too much friction, so I’d just Google it. I finished every day exhausted, bored and sometimes angry. My isolation and lack of motivation were what put me on that list.

02A second chance

A few weeks later, my old company called, the one where I had spent four years going from QA to test lead. This time they wanted me on a dev team. After some onboarding, and without any experience leading developers, I found myself in charge of a small team of analysts. It was my test lead experience that drove that decision. I knew in that moment that I had to change something and work on my people skills. I thrived in that position: I got things done on time, had a good relationship with my team and received high praise and recognition from my client. I thought I had learned the lesson.

03No titles to lean on

Then I joined Kaizen. It’s a horizontal company: no bosses, no titles to lean on. Every decision has to be earned through conversation. I started on a small team where, before long, I found myself defending architecture decisions I had never implemented, against people who had. People started saying yes just to avoid arguing with me. At the time, I took their silence as agreement. I thought I was winning. Some colleagues even joked that I had become a know-it-all. They weren’t really joking.

Like many kids raised in the 90s, I was taught to solve things on my own. Asking for help felt like weakness. So I made sure I always had an answer, right or not.

My arrogance wasn’t confidence. It was the only way I knew to avoid asking for help.

04Breaking the armor

I’d be lying if I said I had an epiphany. It took me a while to realize that even though I was surrounded by people, I was alone. First, I had to get through the shame of having become that arrogant person. Then, I had to break the armor and let people in, to be vulnerable enough to accept help. I needed help. I needed guidance. And I needed to stop solving everything on my own.

Years later, on a night out with some colleagues, one of them told me that when we first met, he couldn’t stand me. Too arrogant. It took him a while to get to know me, and to actually like me. He wasn’t the only one.

It hurt to hear. But it also meant something had changed: he could tell me because I wasn’t that person anymore.

05Where I am today

Today, I enjoy debate. I genuinely believe that one of the most precious gifts people can give me is their opinion on the things we care about. I take in other people’s views and leave room in mine to be shaped by new experiences.

Of course, I still fall back on old habits. When I see something unresolved, I jump in and fix it myself, afraid it won’t get done on time, instead of building the kind of trust where someone can tell me why they couldn’t finish it. And sometimes I swing to the other extreme: I keep an opinion to myself because I’m afraid that defending it will turn me back into that person.

06Why I’m writing this

That’s why I’m writing this blog. To build my own opinions, get comfortable sharing them without the armor, and find out who I am beyond my job title.

Here you’ll find stories from my personal life, from leading teams, mentoring people and bringing AI into my team. I’m not writing to tell anyone how things should be done. Everything here is up for debate, and that’s the point. Sometimes other people’s stories help us get unstuck, or make us want to tell our own. If one of mine does that for you, I’d love to hear it. And if you think I got it wrong, even better.

Thanks for reading. I’m Martín.

I lead teams for clients in the US, coach people, and bring AI into how we work. This blog is where I think out loud.
