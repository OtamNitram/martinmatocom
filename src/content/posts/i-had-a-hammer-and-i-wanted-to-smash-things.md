## A new hammer

Ten years ago, I was sitting in front of my computer, looking proudly at the Angular 2 certification I had just earned. I felt all the power of this new tool and knowledge, and almost immediately I had the urge to start building something. What? Anything. It didn’t matter. I had a hammer and I wanted to smash things with it.

So I started using Angular to build new tools: a website to organize my books and remember who I had lent them to, a tool to graph my habits, and a dozen more. I never published any of them, only a few reached completion, and most were abandoned the moment I lost interest or imagined a new tool. It looked like wasted time, but it wasn’t: every problem I got stuck on taught me something about the framework. **The tools were just the excuse; the learning was the real product.**

## Back to the old repos

A few weeks ago, feeling a bit nostalgic in this new AI era, I went through my old GitHub repos, which still hold dozens of POCs, experiments and random code. With AI as my new hammer, I looked for one of those dead ideas to revive, hoping that now that building is cheap, one of them could become something worth publishing and sharing. Almost immediately, I realized none of them excited me enough to pick it up and finish it. The same reasons I had abandoned those ideas became the reasons I wasn’t interested in reviving any of them. AI could let me experiment fast, discard fast and find out which ideas were worth it, but those ideas had already been filtered, by hand, years ago. And whatever they had to teach me, I had already learned.

## Everyone has a hammer now

But I’m not the only one with a new hammer. Today, anyone can turn an idea into a skill or an agent in minutes, and it feels as rewarding as it did ten years ago. The difference is that these ideas don’t die in a forgotten repo. They show up in team channels and shared repositories, passed from one person to the next, until they become part of the daily work of real teams. I even found myself sharing skills and setting up agents for my own team to use.

Don’t get me wrong: sharing is vital in a team. The problem is ending up with three or four skills to review a PR, some of them mine, each with a different approach, plus a few more to write code, a few more to create test cases, a few more to create tickets. It’s like duplicating, or even triplicating, the team: more members, each with their own approach and their own way of doing things.

And every one of them needs to be maintained.

## Solving the wrong problem

My first instinct was to fix it the way engineers fix everything: with another tool. I set up indexed, AI-ready documentation, so any agent could read from it and be guided to the right skill. I wrote more documentation and ran a few workshops. All of that helped a little, but it was never the solution. The number of tools was growing faster than we could index or document them. It was only when I ran out of options that I stopped, took a few steps back and focused on the problem. **I was solving the wrong problem.** It wasn’t about finding the right skill. The problem was that there were three or four doing the same thing, and I never checked whether any of them solved a real-world problem.

One thing I wonder: if learning is a crucial part of building and discarding, is the amount we learn keeping up with the amount we build and discard? If we are so focused on the how, we can forget the why.

## I still fall into it

Looking back, building was never the hard part. Knowing what deserved to be built was. These days, I still use AI as my hammer, but I try to point it at the problem first. A couple of times, without much formality, I’ve had agents watch someone navigate a prototype, to refine the idea or discard it.

And I still fall into it. While playing Diablo IV, I built a skill to analyze my inventory and suggest improvements for my build. The entire point of playing a game is to have fun, but since I had the hammer, I turned my game into some kind of work project.

Just because I can doesn’t mean it’s useful, or even a good idea.

## Every agent earns its place

I’ve been thinking about this a lot while working on agentic software factories, where you can’t afford three or four agents doing the same thing. And not only that: I’ve caught myself adding extra steps to the pipeline just because it’s easy, when in reality those steps don’t solve any problem. I need to pay extra attention when I build my own software factories, or help someone else build one. Having an agent that can solve a particular problem doesn’t make it a problem the factory needs to solve. But how to build a factory where every agent earns its place is a story for another post.

I’m really curious whether you also feel overwhelmed by the amount of AI tooling around.
